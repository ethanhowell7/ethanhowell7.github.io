About

Where I come from, how I make decisions, and where this is going.

I'm a first-year electrical engineering student at NC State University, in the University Honors Program. I grew up in Andover, Massachusetts, and split my time between Raleigh during the school year and Andover over the summers.

Outside of class I hold a black belt in karate and have taught it for several years, I spent four years in competitive FTC robotics — three as mechanical lead on ARC Lightning, then a year as team leader of ARC Sandstorm — and I run a self-employed DJ business around Greater Boston. Electrical engineering is what stuck after AP Physics C and a lot of time soldering guitar pedals: I like problems where you can see and measure what's happening, and where being wrong usually tells you exactly how you were wrong.

How I decide things

Five principles from martial arts training. They're most useful in gray areas — situations where more than one reasonable value is in tension and there's no obviously correct answer.

Effort

Showing up consistently matters more than any single result, whether that's a match or a competition deadline.

Etiquette

How you treat people when you don't have to says more about your standards than claiming them does.

Sincerity

Give a straightforward answer, even when a vaguer one would be easier in the moment.

Self-control

Staying level under pressure — a match, a deadline, a circuit that won't behave — is a practiced skill, not a trait.

Character

What you do when nobody is watching or scoring it is the real measure.

The clearest example: as team leader of ARC Sandstorm, choosing to build up newer members' skills rather than routing every hard problem through the most experienced people, even though it cost us ground competitively in the short term.

Where this is going

Near term: a summer 2027 engineering internship, and finding my way into semiconductor device research here at NC State. Further out I'm drawn to power electronics and device work specifically — the corner of EE where a small design decision has a direct physical consequence you can measure.
