Leading a team, teaching a skill, working with kids, running an organization, running a business. Each one is a progression, so each is told in order.

Four years of FTC robotics

Two teams, two different jobs: three years learning the engineering side, then a year responsible for the people doing it.

Freshman – junior year

ARC Lightning — mechanical team, then mechanical lead

Three seasons on an established team, working on the robot's mechanical design and build and growing into the mechanical lead role. Our junior-year robot went to the FTC World Championship — a team result I was one part of.

Senior year

ARC Sandstorm — team leader

Moving from mechanical lead to team leader meant the hard problems stopped being technical. I led the team to states and the New England Invitational, ran "ARC Election '26" for team leadership positions using both simple and ranked-choice ballots, and closed the season with a banquet and nature-themed awards.

The decision I keep coming back to: spending time building up newer members' skills instead of routing every hard problem through our most experienced people. Slower, and it probably cost us ground early on — but it's the only version where the team still works after I've graduated.

Karate is where most of my approach to coaching comes from — and where the teaching-meets-engineering project below happened.

Training

Black belt

Years of training at Cervizzi's Martial Arts Academy, through to black belt. The rank matters less to me than what it took to get there — showing up consistently, and getting better at judging my own form without waiting to be corrected.

Several years

Senior instructor

My job as an instructor isn't really getting a student to copy a technique correctly — it's giving them a way to judge their own form, so they keep improving without me standing over their shoulder. That's the same thing I was trying to do leading Sandstorm.

I also handle purchasing for the academy — equipment, inventory, the ordering side of running a dojo — which is a different kind of responsibility than teaching, and one I picked up because someone needed to.

Summer camp

Designing a STEM curriculum for the dojo

The one place my two worlds fully overlapped.

Featured work

Four-day robotics-and-karate camp

I designed and ran the camp from scratch: daily lesson plans on solar energy, gears, biomimicry, and the engineering design loop, each with its own slide deck and diagrams I drew myself.

Building the lesson material took considerably longer than running the camp did — probably true of teaching in general. It's still the project that best represents how I think about both fields at once.

Volunteering

Started as a volunteer

Before it was a job, it was a place I showed up to help. That's how I got to know the center, the staff, and the kids well enough to be trusted with more.

Building monitor

Keeping the space running

First point of contact for whoever walked in, and the person responsible for the building being safe and orderly when nobody else was watching. Unglamorous, and a good test of whether you can be relied on.

Camp counselor

Running days for kids

Full days with a group of kids — planning, supervising, and keeping things moving. It's where I learned that the energy in a room comes from the person leading it, whether they mean it to or not.

Weightlifting coach

Teaching kids to lift safely

Coaching young lifters on form and progression — the same job as karate instruction in a different room: give them the judgment to know when a rep is right, not just tell them.

Inducted

Member

Selected on academics, service, leadership, and character — the standard NHS criteria. Membership itself is a recognition; what I found more interesting was what happens after.

Executive board

Helping run a 200-person organization

At that scale, leadership is mostly logistics: keeping service hours, meetings, and events organized well enough that two hundred people can actually participate instead of just being on a roster. It's a different skill from leading a robotics team of a dozen, and it taught me that clear systems are a form of respect for people's time.

Starting out

First events

It started as playing events and figuring out the gear. What it turned into was learning to book, quote, and show up prepared — the part nobody sees from the dance floor.

Growing it

Treating it like a business

Pricing the work properly, managing clients, keeping equipment and travel costs from quietly eating a slow month, and being reliable enough that people book again. Running a business is mostly the unglamorous parts done consistently.

Now

Still running it

Active around Greater Boston, mostly over summers and breaks now that I'm in Raleigh during the school year. Same instinct I'd bring to an engineering problem, pointed at a calendar and a client list instead of a circuit.
