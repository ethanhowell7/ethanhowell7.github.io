Guitar pedal builds

Hardware
Role: Builder Status: Ongoing

Pedals are where I first got comfortable with analog circuits outside a textbook. I solder from schematics and spend most of the time afterward working out why a circuit isn't doing what the diagram says it should — which is where the real learning is.

This is also where guitar and engineering overlap for me. I play electric guitar, and building the effects I use means every design decision has an audible consequence. It's taught me more about how transistors and op-amps behave under real conditions than any lecture so far.

Robotics
Team: FTC #24256 Season: Senior year Reached: New England Invitational

Our senior-year robot, built and iterated across a full FTC season. As team leader I was less hands-on with the mechanism than I'd been on my previous team, and more responsible for making sure the build actually converged — scoping what we'd attempt, keeping it testable, and reworking what failed first.

ARC Lightning competition robots

Robotics
Team: FTC #4410 Seasons: Freshman – junior Reached: FTC World Championship

Three seasons on the mechanical side of an established team. I contributed to the design and build of each year's robot and moved into the mechanical lead role as I went — though these were team robots, and the credit is shared.

Our junior-year robot went to the FTC World Championship. What I took from those three years was less any one mechanism than the habit of iterating: build a version, test it against the actual game rules, and rework what fails first.

Fretboard trainer

Software
Language: Python Status: Done

A small app for drilling note positions on the guitar neck — built because I wanted one, and because it was a good excuse to write a real program end to end.

The interesting part wasn't the code so much as the design question: how do you quiz someone on a two-dimensional layout in a way that actually builds intuition rather than memorization?

Guitar type personality quiz

Software
Language: Java Status: Done

A branching quiz that matches the person taking it to a style of guitar. Not a serious tool — it was a way to get comfortable with Java's structure and control flow while making something with a bit of personality.

If you're curious, it's a decent snapshot of what "learning a language by building something you'd actually use" looked like for me.

Semiconductor device research

Research
Interest: SiC / GaN & silicon devices Where: NC State ECE Status: Working toward

I'm working to get involved in wide-bandgap and silicon device research within NC State's ECE department, which plays a central role in the CHIPS-funded CLAWS Microelectronics Commons Hub and in PowerAmerica.

Power devices are the corner of EE I find most interesting right now: a small change in a device's design has a direct, physical consequence you can measure. This card turns into a real writeup once the work starts.

Middle school science fair

Early work

Two apps a friend and I built for science fair, years before any of the above. I'm including them because they're where the pattern started: building something for a specific person who needed it.

[App name] — an accessible game for students with cerebral palsy

Early work
Built with: A friend For: Students with cerebral palsy

My mom is an occupational therapist, and after hearing about her work with students with cerebral palsy, a friend and I built a game for them to play. It was controlled with a single large button that was easy to press, so the interface itself didn't get in the way.

Cerebral palsy is a spectrum, so the game could be adjusted depending on whether a student was more physically or more cognitively challenged. That adjustability was the whole point — the same game had to work for very different kids. It was the first time I designed something around a constraint I couldn't see from my own chair.

Buddiey

Early work
Built with: The same friend For: Seniors living alone

A conversational companion aimed at senior loneliness and isolation. We built the model ourselves — this was before AI was something everyone talked about, which meant working out from scratch how a program could hold up its end of a conversation.

Looking back, the ambition outran the tools we had. But the problem it was aimed at is real and still unsolved, and it was an early lesson that the hard part of a technical project is usually deciding what it's actually for.
